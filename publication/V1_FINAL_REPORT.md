## 1. What was built

A graph in which **nodes are places on the Indian railway network and edges are
physical track links between adjacent places**. This is the *space of stations*
in the terminology of Kurant & Thiran (2006) — the physical layer, as distinct
from a timetable-derived network where two stations are linked if a train serves
both.

| | |
|---|---|
| nodes | **9,095** — 8,621 stations, 474 operational junctions |
| edges | **9,689** physical track links |
| total route km | **72,201** |
| connected components | 18 — one giant (9,058 nodes, **99.6%**) and 17 small |
| articulation points | 1,338 (14.8% of nodes) |
| bridges (cut edges) | 1,412 (14.6% of edges) |
| independent cycles | 612 — meshedness 0.034, so the network is 93.7% tree-like |
| degree | mean **2.13**, maximum **7** |
| zones represented | 17 |

For scale: the two established graph studies of this network used **587 stations**
(Sen et al. 2003) and **809** (Bhatia et al. 2015), neither at the physical layer
and neither released as a reusable artifact.

> **Figure 1** — `publication/figures/fig1_network.png`: the network at true
> coordinates, stations in blue and operational junctions in orange.

### The hybrid node model

Not every topology point is a station. Where corridors meet at a place that could
not be confidently matched to a station in the master list, the graph keeps an
**operational junction** node (`JN::` prefix) rather than discarding the topology
or inventing a station. 474 of 9,095 nodes are of this kind. This is `D-001`, and
it is why the graph is connected at all.

## 2. How it was built

Ten pipeline stages turn GIS polylines into a station graph. Four steps carry the
weight:

**1 · Corridors.** 119,446 GIS polyline segments are grouped into **2,305
corridors** by their declared junction endpoints.

**2 · Spine reconstruction.** The segments of a corridor are merged into one
ordered polyline — the *spine* — and every station is projected onto it. A
station is kept if its perpendicular offset is ≤1.5 km (`D-004`). Its position
along the spine is recorded as `measure_km`.

**3 · Edge construction.** Consecutive placed stations become an edge. Length is
the **haversine chord** between their coordinates, floored at 0.05 km. **(Corrected by `F-042`: the v1.0 length was already the along-spine measure on non-stitch edges and the chord only on stitch edges; graph v1.1 ships the `D-026` hybrid with `length_source` per edge.)**

**4 · Junction stitching (`D-005`) — the novel operator.** Corridor chains alone
give a graph that is only **59.4% connected**, because corridors meet each other
at junctions rather than at stations. Attaching each chain to its junction
endpoints raises connectivity to **99.6%**. 2,252 edges (23.2%) are stitched.

Junction stitching is the construction contribution of this work. Its second
property is more interesting than its first: it **damps the graph's sensitivity to
the placement tolerance** — but by wildly different factors depending on what you
measure (§6.3).

### Reproducibility

The whole build is parameterised by a frozen 12-field `BuildSpec`, hashed to a
short identifier. `railgraph` reimplements the Phase 2 construction rather than
modifying it (`D-023`), and an equivalence test confirms on **every run** that it
reproduces all 9,689 edges with an identical pair set and zero length mismatches.
Every output is content-hashed in a manifest alongside its inputs and library
versions.

## 6. What we learned that is scientifically interesting

### 6.1 Sinuosity recovers India's mountain railways with no terrain input

Phase 2 computed each station's position along the corridor spine and the graph
then ignored it, storing straight chords instead. Differencing those positions
gives the true along-track distance. The ratio of the two — **sinuosity** — ranks
871 corridors like this:

| rank | corridor | sinuosity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Matheran ↔ Neral | 2.653 | Matheran Hill Railway |
| **2** | Darjeeling ↔ New Jalpaiguri | 2.007 | **UNESCO 1999** |
| **3** | Kalka ↔ Shimla | 1.875 | **UNESCO 2008** |
| 4 | Padua ↔ Shrungavarapukota | 1.688 | Araku ghat |
| 5 | Barkhera ↔ Budni | 1.643 | Budni ghat |
| **6** | Mettupalayam ↔ Udagamandalam | 1.509 | **UNESCO 2005** |

**All four of India's recognised mountain railways in the top 6 of 871 — the top
0.7%** — from a pipeline containing no elevation, gradient, terrain or heritage
data of any kind. The rest of the top twelve is Bhor Ghat, Braganza Ghat, the
Kashmir link and the Bastar hills.

This is the best validation in the project, because the ground truth comes from
entirely outside the data and could not have been gamed. It also converts the
chord-length shortcut from a rounding complaint into a substantive one: the chord
does not merely shorten distances, it **erases the topography that defines those
corridors**. *(Figure 7)*

### 6.2 Random failure is worse than targeted attack

Percolation under four strategies gives an **inverted** ordering relative to the
textbook scale-free result: random failure damages this network more than
betweenness-targeted attack. The same code on a Barabási–Albert graph reproduces
the expected ordering, so it is the network, not the implementation. The reason is
structural — high-betweenness nodes lie *contiguously along trunk routes* rather
than acting as separable hubs, so removing them is removing a stretch of one line.

Holds in **19 of 20** sensitivity cells, so Module 3 can state it as a property of
Indian Railways rather than a caveat about our build. *(Figure 4)*

### 6.3 Stitching's damping is measure-specific, and saying so honestly matters

Junction stitching nearly eliminates the graph's sensitivity to the placement
tolerance — but by factors spanning two and a half orders of magnitude:

| measure | damping |
|---|---|
| giant component share | **322×** |
| mean shortest path | 30× |
| bridge share | 25× |
| top-50 corridor criticality | 3.3× |
| R, random attack | 2.3× |
| meshedness | **1.3×** |

"Our operator makes results insensitive to a free parameter" would have been an
overclaim. The per-measure version is more useful anyway: it tells a reader which
of *their* results the technique protects, and cycle-structure work still has to
justify its tolerance. *(Figure 6)*

### 6.4 A validation that demoted its own attribute

The GIS splits its polylines at structures, so bridge flags are genuine
segment-level data — 57,388 structures, median length 22.7 m against 686 m for
unflagged segments. It looked like the best new attribute available.

Validated against the official per-zone bridge count, it captures **31.6%**, with
capture ranging **0.006 in Northeast Frontier Railway to 0.638 in Western
Railway**. Demoted to within-zone relative use only (`D-028`), or dropped if that
restriction cannot be enforced downstream. A zone-correlated bias inside a
resilience result is worse than no attribute at all. *(Figure 9)*

### 6.5 An invented constant outperformed a real attribute

The graph weights travel time by `speed_kmph`, the declared sectional speed. The
timetable data gives an observed run-time on 66.9% of edges — **better coverage
than the declared field**. Trains achieve a median **0.513** of the declared
speed, so travel times are out by about 2×.

And where the declared field is absent, the code substitutes a flat 50 km/h. The
observed median is **53.5 km/h**. *The constant we invented is closer to reality
than the real attribute*, so an edge with a declared speed is weighted worse than
one without — and no coverage statistic would ever reveal it, because coverage
counts presence, not correctness. *(Figure 8)*

### 6.6 Naming is a provenance surface

Three independent instances of the same failure in one dataset:

- `is_colocated` means "this length was floored", not "these stations are
  co-located" (`F-002`).
- `speed_kmph` is a sanctioned maximum, not an achieved speed (`F-036`).
- `service_direct` and `service_load` are observation and inference respectively,
  differing on 36.5% of edges — and on 1,031 edges `direct = 0` while `load > 0`
  (`F-034`).

In each case the name asserts more than the value supports, and in each case a
downstream user would have been misled without reading the source. A dataset's
column names are part of its provenance, not labels on top of it.

## 7. What is wrong with it

Stated here rather than left for a reviewer. Full treatment in
`publication/GAP_ANALYSIS.md`.

### 7.1 The node set is incomplete — **critical**

The GIS declares **21,560** station placements across the corridors used; the
pipeline matched **12,305**. A **42.9% shortfall**. Where a station was not
placed, the builder does not create two short edges through it — it creates **one
long edge spanning the gap**.

| | |
|---|---|
| edges with ≥3 stations unmatched **and** longer than 10 km | **766 (7.9%)** |
| route km they carry | **17,722 — 24.5% of the network** |
| of those, with no train traversing them as one hop | **40.9%** |

`MYSORE JN → YESVANTPUR JN` is a 125.6 km edge on a corridor whose spine is 1.4
km. A 136 km single hop is a shortcut in every path computation: it shortens
paths, misplaces betweenness, deflates circuity, and makes the network look
better connected and more redundant than it is.

**Honest bound:** `noofstations` is itself an unaudited corridor constant, so some
shortfall is legitimate (goods points, closed halts, stations outside our master).
42.9% is an upper bound on error; the 766-edge defect subset is the defensible
core. *(Figure 2 maps them — they are national, not localised.)*

### 7.2 Four of six physical attributes are corridor constants — **major**

`gauge_code`, `speed_kmph`, `n_tracks` and `zone_code` are constant within a
corridor in **96–99%** of corridors. **119,446 source segments carry 2,305
distinct values.** Two edges on the same corridor are not two observations, so
edge-level statistics over these fields are overconfident by roughly the
edge-to-corridor ratio. Only the bridge flag is genuinely per-segment.

### 7.3 Lengths are chords — **major**

Aggregate understatement is only 2.88%, but **17.7% of station pairs are
understated by more than 5%**, 8.6% by more than 10%, and the error concentrates
exactly where track curves. The fix is not a simple swap: the along-track measure
is itself physically impossible on 12.9% of pairs, because stations far from the
spine project onto the same point. The failure is monotonic in offset — 1.8%
broken below 0.1 km, **81.8% in the 1.0–1.5 km band** that `D-004` admits. Hence
a gated hybrid with recorded provenance (`D-026`).

### 7.4 A third of travel times are a constant we chose — **major**
### 7.5 `state` is 45.8% and not recoverable in-repo — **major for accessibility work**

Both §6.5 and `F-033`. The station master holds exactly the same 48.3% the graph
does; the fix is reverse geocoding from the coordinates, which are complete.

### 7.6 Model-level scope

Undirected (railways are not), atemporal, uncapacitated, no electrification, and
parallel routes collapsed — on 124 edges the discarded parallel carried genuinely
different attributes, which is real redundancy a resilience study should not throw
away.

|---|---|

## 8. What it is and is not safe for

**Safe:** topological and structural analysis; connectivity, components and cut
structure; community detection; criticality at **corridor** granularity;
resilience and percolation on the physical layer; comparative work against other
national rail networks.

**Not safe without v1.1:** precise distance or travel-time results; anything
weighted by `speed_kmph`; edge-level statistical inference on the corridor-constant
attributes; absolute or cross-zone bridge counts; directional analysis; capacity
or congestion modelling; freight (the timetable source is 52 goods trains of
5,208).

**Citation posture:** cite v1.0 for structural results at corridor granularity;
wait for v1.1 before publishing distance- or travel-time-weighted findings.

## 10. The methods lessons

For whoever picks this up, these generalise past this dataset.

**1 · A check that asserts non-nullity cannot detect an invented value.**
`VERIFICATION.txt` passed its "no null edge lengths" test *because* an undefined
haversine had been floored into a number. Assert that a value was **measured**,
not that it is present.

**2 · Export to a stricter format as a validation technique.** The `JN::Khandala`
defect was found by GeoJSON refusing to invent a coordinate. No internal check
caught it. Round-tripping through a format with stronger requirements than your
own is a cheap, genuine test.

**3 · Joining to segments does not make an attribute segment-level.** The join
looks like it produces per-segment data and silently does not. Measure the
granularity at which a value actually varies before you store it per edge.

**4 · Validate before you ship an attractive attribute.** The bridge inventory
looked excellent and validation demoted it. An addition with no failure mode is an
assertion, not a measurement.

**5 · Coverage counts presence, not correctness.** A field present on 64% of rows
can be worse than a constant, and no completeness metric will tell you.

**6 · Tier your robustness verdicts.** A single verdict over every variation said
"everything is an artifact", which only restated that the two load-bearing choices
were load-bearing. Conditioning on *which* choices are genuinely open turned a
non-result into the project's main result.

**7 · Report the validations that failed.** Two of ours did — the bridge capture
rate and the route-km reconciliation. A validation section containing only
confirmations is not a validation section.
